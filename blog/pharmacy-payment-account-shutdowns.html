Why Your Pharmacy's Payment Account Got Shut Down (and How to Make Sure It Never Happens Again)

Derek Fite, APP Merchant Services

It usually starts with an email.

No phone call, no warning, no real conversation. Just a notice that your account's been deactivated, your ability to accept cards is gone as of right now, and a chunk of your funds will be held for the next 90 to 180 days while the platform "completes its review."

If you run a licensed online pharmacy or telehealth business, there's a decent chance this has already happened to you. If it hasn't, understanding why it happens to businesses like yours is the best way to make sure it never does.

The uncomfortable math behind the shutdowns

Here's the reality every pharmacy operator lives with: the vast majority of websites selling medication online aren't operating legally. The National Association of Boards of Pharmacy reviewed more than 11,000 online pharmacy websites and found that roughly 96% failed to meet state and federal law or patient safety standards. They sell without valid prescriptions, ship unapproved products, or operate from outside U.S. jurisdiction entirely.

You already know this. It's exactly why you spent months and real money getting licensed in every state you ship to, earning your certifications, and building a compliance program that could survive an inspection.

The problem is that most payment platforms never look that closely. And when the bad actors in a category outnumber the good ones by that much, the cheapest risk decision a platform can make is to avoid the category altogether.

Aggregators were never built for you

The popular payment platforms that power most of online commerce are aggregators. Instead of giving your business its own merchant account, they pool thousands of businesses under one master account and use automated systems to watch for anything that looks unusual.

That model works great for selling t-shirts. It's the wrong model for pharmacy, and here's why.

The category itself gets flagged. Pharmacy sits under merchant category codes that card networks watch more closely. Most mainstream platforms either prohibit pharmaceutical sales in their terms of service or allow them only with prior written approval that almost nobody actually gets. A lot of pharmacy operators are technically violating their platform's terms from the day they sign up, without ever knowing it. The account works fine right up until an automated review notices what the business actually sells.

The monitoring is automated and pattern based. Aggregators watch the master account, not your business specifically. A jump in sales volume, a shift in average ticket size, a seasonal spike, or a modest run of disputes can trigger a review. The system doesn't know you just signed a new telehealth partner. It just knows the pattern changed.

And there's no underwriting relationship to fall back on. Because no human ever reviewed your licenses, your certifications, or your business model in the first place, there's no file anywhere that says "this merchant is legitimate." When the algorithm flags you, there's nothing on the other side of the scale.

None of this makes aggregators bad companies. It just makes them the wrong tool for a regulated, card-not-present healthcare business.

What a shutdown actually costs

The obvious cost is the revenue gap. A pharmacy that loses card acceptance today loses most of its sales today, and getting a properly underwritten replacement account up and running isn't an overnight process, especially if you're starting the paperwork from zero on the worst day of your quarter.

The held funds hurt more than most operators expect going in. Platforms commonly hold reserves for months after a termination to cover potential disputes, which means a real slice of your working capital is frozen exactly when you need it most to keep inventory moving and payroll on time.

The quieter, longer-term cost is the record it leaves behind. Terminations for cause can land a business in the card networks' shared databases of terminated merchants, which every acquiring bank checks during underwriting. One bad exit can follow your business for years and make every future application harder. This is the strongest reason to fix your processing setup before a shutdown happens, not after.

The warning signs, in order

Shutdowns feel sudden. They almost never actually are. Watch for this progression:

  1. Funding delays. Deposits that used to arrive next day start taking two or three days, with no real explanation given.
  2. Requests for information. The platform asks for invoices, fulfillment records, or supplier details. A review is already underway at this point.
  3. A rolling reserve appears. A percentage of each sale starts getting held back. The platform now considers you a risk it's pricing for.
  4. Partial holds. A payout gets skipped or reduced pending review.

If you're seeing steps one or two right now, the time to line up properly underwritten processing is today, while your account is still open and you can move on your own schedule instead of the platform's.

What processing that actually lasts looks like

The stable version of pharmacy payments isn't some secret formula. It's a dedicated merchant account, underwritten up front by people who actually understand the category.

That means a real human reviews your state licenses, your certifications, your prescribing and fulfillment model, and your processing history before you're approved. It's more work at the start. It's also exactly why properly underwritten accounts don't vanish by email one morning: everything about your business was known and accepted on day one, so normal growth and normal fluctuations don't read as red flags.

It also means the compliance requirements that apply to every pharmacy merchant, things like third-party certification and card network registration, get handled by someone who actually knows what they're looking at and explains it up front. These are industry-wide standards that follow your business no matter who processes your payments. The real difference between providers is whether anyone bothers to tell you about them before they become a problem.

And it means reserve terms that are put in writing, tied to your actual chargeback record, and scheduled to shrink as your account matures, instead of appearing out of nowhere as a defensive reaction.

If you've already done the hard part, the licensing, the certification, the compliance program, then stable online pharmacy payment processing isn't a favor anyone's doing you. It's what your file has already earned.

The five-minute version

Every licensed pharmacy and telehealth operator should be able to answer three questions:

  1. Does my current payment provider know, in writing, exactly what my business sells?
  2. Was my account individually underwritten, or am I pooled under a platform's master account?
  3. If my account closed tomorrow morning, how many days of revenue would I lose before a replacement was live?
