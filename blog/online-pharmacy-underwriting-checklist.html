The Online Pharmacy Underwriting Checklist: Every Document You'll Need, In Order

Derek Fite, APP Merchant Services

Ask five different payment providers what they need to approve a pharmacy merchant account, and you'll probably get five different answers, delivered five different ways, over five different weeks.

That's not because pharmacy underwriting is unusually complicated. It's because most providers don't actually know what they need until they've already started reviewing your file, so they ask for one thing, wait, look at what came back, then ask for the next thing. Every round trip costs you days. For a licensed pharmacy or telehealth business trying to get processing live, that's the real cost of a badly run application: not the paperwork itself, but the drip.

Here's the full list, in the order a reviewer actually needs it, so you can gather it once instead of five times.

1. Business formation documents

Start here because everything else gets checked against it. Your articles of incorporation or organization, your EIN confirmation letter, and a current certificate of good standing from your state of formation. If you operate under a name that's different from your legal entity name, bring your DBA filing too.

This step is boring and it matters. A mismatch between your legal entity name and the name on your pharmacy license is one of the most common things that stalls a file in the first week.

2. State pharmacy board license

Your home state license, plus every nonresident pharmacy license for the states you ship into. If you're licensed in twenty states, bring all twenty. A reviewer wants to see the full footprint, not just a sample, because your nonresident licensing is what tells them how big and how legitimate your shipping operation actually is.

If any license is up for renewal within the next few months, mention that upfront. It's a normal thing to flag and it's much better coming from you than discovered later.

3. LegitScript certification

This is the single document that moves your file the fastest. Card networks require it for card-not-present pharmacy and telehealth merchants, so if you already have it, lead with it. If you're mid-application with LegitScript, say so and share your application status.

4. NABP accreditation or .pharmacy domain, if you have either

Not every legitimate pharmacy holds these, so don't worry if you don't. But if you do, include them. They're not required the way LegitScript effectively is, but they strengthen a file and can speed up review, since they represent independent confirmation of exactly the things underwriting is trying to verify anyway.

5. DEA registration, but only if it applies to you

This one trips people up because it's not a universal requirement. DEA registration governs controlled substances specifically. If your business dispenses controlled medications, this is a required document. If it doesn't, and a lot of GLP-1 and weight management telehealth models don't, then you can skip this step entirely and move on. Knowing which category you're in before you start gathering documents saves you from chasing paperwork you don't actually need.

6. A written description of your prescribing and fulfillment model

This is the document most applicants forget, and it's often the one that speeds everything else up. Write a page that plainly explains how a patient gets a prescription (in-house prescribers, a telehealth partner, patient-submitted prescriptions from outside providers), who fills it, and how it ships. If you're a telehealth platform that doesn't dispense yourself, this is where you explain your pharmacy partnership. A reviewer who understands your model in five minutes asks fewer follow-up questions later.

7. Processing history, if you have any

Three to six months of statements from a previous processor, if you've processed before. This isn't about proving you've never had a problem. It's about giving underwriting real numbers to work with instead of guesses, since your actual chargeback rate and average ticket size are what eventually set your reserve terms. If a previous account was terminated, bring that up directly rather than hoping it doesn't come up. Terminations from mainstream aggregators are common in this category and usually say more about the platform's blanket policy than about your business, but underwriters need to hear the explanation from you, not find it on their own.

8. Refund, cancellation, and billing policies

Especially important if you run subscription billing for auto-refills or ongoing GLP-1 treatment. A written policy that covers refunds, how patients cancel, and how billing descriptors appear on statements does double duty: it satisfies underwriting and it's the same document that keeps your dispute rate low once you're live, since a lot of pharmacy chargebacks come from patients who forgot they were on a recurring plan and didn't know how to cancel easily.

9. Website terms and privacy policy

Your site needs both, and they need to actually describe what your business does. A privacy policy copied from a template that doesn't mention your industry, or a terms page still referencing a different business model, is a small thing that reads as a bigger red flag than it should.

10. Ownership and identification documents

Government-issued ID for each owner with 25% or greater ownership, and basic KYC information: date of birth, address, ownership percentage. This is standard across essentially all merchant underwriting, not pharmacy-specific, but it's often the step people forget because it feels unrelated to the rest of the file.

11. Banking information

A voided check or bank letter for the account you want funded. Simple, but make sure the account name matches your legal entity name from step one. Another common, easily avoided delay.

Why the order matters

Notice that the list moves from "who you are" to "what you're licensed to do" to "how your business actually works" to "your track record" to "the mechanics of getting paid." That's not arbitrary. It's the order a human reviewer's questions naturally follow, and gathering documents in that order means you're rarely digging for something a step behind what's already been asked.

If you're building your file before you've been asked for anything at all, even better. Merchants who arrive at online pharmacy payment processing with this list already assembled tend to move through underwriting noticeably faster than merchants gathering documents reactively, one email at a time.

What "clean" looks like

A quick gut check for each document before you submit it: is the business name consistent everywhere, are all your licenses current rather than expired or pending renewal, and does your written prescribing and fulfillment description match what your website actually says you do. Most of the delays that feel like underwriting being slow are actually small inconsistencies like these getting caught and needing a round of clarification. Catching them yourself before you submit is the single fastest thing you can do to shorten the process.

Start before you need to

You don't have to wait until you're actively being onboarded to put this list together. If you're watching for the warning signs of a shutdown with your current processor, or you're a newer pharmacy building your compliance program from the ground up, assembling this file now means you're ready to move the moment you need to, instead of starting from zero on the worst possible day.
